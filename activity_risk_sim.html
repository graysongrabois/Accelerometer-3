How This All Works — Plain English

This guide explains what we built, why we built it, and what's wrong with it. No prerequisites needed — if you can understand a recipe, you can understand this.

1. What Data Are We Using?

The data comes from NHANES — the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. Every few years, the CDC recruits thousands of Americans and does a full health checkup: blood tests, measurements, questionnaires. In 2005, they also handed out a device called an accelerometer.

What is an accelerometer?

An accelerometer is a tiny sensor that detects movement. You've got one in your phone — it's how your phone knows when you tilt it. The ones in this study were worn on the hip for 7 days straight, recording how much you moved every single minute.

From that minute-by-minute motion data, we can calculate things like: How many steps did you take? How long did you sit completely still? Did you sleep through the night?

The catch: This is only 7 days of data per person. A single week is not representative of someone's activity over months or years. That's one of the big reasons this model is limited.

Who is in the dataset?

We used 1,516 adults age 50 and older. Of those, 238 (15.7%) reported being hospitalized in the past year. The accelerometry data was recorded at the same time as — or after — the hospitalization, which matters a lot (more on this later).

2. What Features Did We Build?

The raw data is 15 million rows of minute-level motion readings. We can't feed that directly into a model — we need to summarize it into a few meaningful numbers per person. These summaries are called features.

Total Steps (weekly)

The total number of steps taken across all 7 wear days. Simple and intuitive. Population median: about 38,000 steps/week (roughly 5,400/day).

% Sedentary Time (pct_zero)

The fraction of all recorded minutes where the accelerometer detected zero movement, expressed as a percentage. Someone who sits at a desk all day might be 75% sedentary. Someone who walks a lot might be 50% sedentary. The population median in NHANES adults 50+ is about 68%.

Sleep Proxy

The fraction of nighttime minutes (10pm–6am) where the device recorded zero movement. A value close to 1.0 means you were essentially still all night — which usually means you were sleeping. A lower value might mean you were restless, got up frequently, or were actually active at night.

Tricky interpretation: A high sleep proxy (close to 1.0) is associated with lower hospitalization risk in our model. But this might be backwards — sick people often sleep poorly, so "bad sleep proxy" might just be a marker for being sick, not a cause of hospitalization.

BMI (Body Mass Index)

Your weight in kilograms divided by your height in meters squared. It's an imperfect but widely-used proxy for body fat. Categories: Under 18.5 = underweight, 18.5–25 = healthy, 25–30 = overweight, 30+ = obese. BMI turned out to be the strongest predictor in our model — stronger than any of the accelerometry features.

Activity Slope

How your step count changed across the 7 days. Did you walk more on day 7 than day 1, or less? This was supposed to be the "trajectory" feature — capturing physical decline. It had no significant effect (p=0.504). With only 7 days, there's not enough data to detect meaningful trends.

3. What is a Cox Proportional Hazards Model?

A Cox model is a type of survival analysis. Survival analysis was originally designed to answer questions like "how long until a patient dies?" — but it works for any time-to-event question: time until hospitalization, time until recovery, time until a machine fails.

The basic idea

Imagine you're watching 1,516 people over time. Some of them get hospitalized. The Cox model asks: at any given moment, how does each person's risk of being hospitalized right now depend on their characteristics?

That "risk at any given moment" is called the hazard. The model says:

Hazard(person) = Baseline Hazard × e^(β₁×Steps + β₂×SedentaryTime + β₃×SleepProxy + β₄×BMI + ...) Where: β₁, β₂, β₃, β₄ = coefficients (weights learned from data) e = Euler's number (~2.718), because we're on a log scale Baseline Hazard = average risk in the population (Cox doesn't estimate this directly)

The key insight is that you don't need to know the baseline hazard to compare two people. The baseline cancels out when you take the ratio. This is the "proportional" in proportional hazards: the ratio of two people's hazards stays constant over time.

What is exp(coef), the hazard ratio?

Each coefficient β gets exponentiated (raised to the power of e) to produce a hazard ratio (HR). The HR tells you:

  • HR = 1.0 → this variable has no effect on risk
  • HR = 1.5 → a one-unit increase in this variable multiplies hazard by 1.5 (50% higher risk)
  • HR = 0.5 → a one-unit increase in this variable cuts hazard in half

Reading the BMI row as an example

BMI coefficient = 0.058. Hazard ratio = e^0.058 = 1.059. That means for every 1 unit increase in BMI, your hospitalization hazard goes up by 5.9%. Going from BMI 25 to BMI 35 (10 units) means: 1.059^10 = 1.78× the hazard — nearly double the risk.

What is the C-index?

The C-index (concordance index) measures how well the model ranks people by risk. Take any two people — one who got hospitalized, one who didn't. What fraction of the time did the model assign higher risk to the hospitalized person?

  • 0.50 = random guessing (coin flip)
  • 0.65+ = clinically useful
  • 1.00 = perfect

Our model got 0.639 — better than chance, but not reliable enough to act on clinically.

4. The Step Count Threshold — What is "Youden-Optimal"?

This one is directly from our notebook (Part 5). The question we asked: What weekly step count best separates people who were hospitalized from people who weren't?

ROC Curve — finding the best cutoff

Imagine you draw a line and say "everyone below this step count is 'high risk.'" If you set the line very low (say, 1,000 steps/week), you'll catch almost every hospitalized person — but you'll also falsely flag almost everyone else as high risk. If you set it very high (80,000 steps/week), you'll barely flag anyone, including many who were actually hospitalized.

The ROC curve plots every possible threshold along a curve, showing the tradeoff between:

  • Sensitivity — out of everyone who actually was hospitalized, what fraction did the threshold catch?
  • Specificity — out of everyone who wasn't hospitalized, what fraction did the threshold correctly leave alone?

A perfect threshold would have 100% sensitivity AND 100% specificity. In reality, improving one usually hurts the other.

Youden's J — picking the best point on the curve

Youden's J statistic is a simple formula for finding the best single threshold on the ROC curve:

J = Sensitivity + Specificity − 1 The threshold where J is highest is the one that balances catching real cases vs. avoiding false alarms as well as possible.

In our notebook, the Youden-optimal threshold for weekly steps came out to approximately 35,000 steps/week. Below that, hospitalization rates in the sample were noticeably higher. Above it, they were lower. The simulator flags this threshold because it's a real number from our actual data — not made up.

The ROC AUC (area under the curve) for total steps alone was about 0.57 — barely better than random (0.50 = coin flip). Steps alone aren't a great predictor. The Cox model combining all features got to C-index 0.639, which is better but still limited.

5. How Does the Simulator Estimate "Expected Hospitalizations Per Year"?

Honest disclaimer: The Cox model in the notebook does not directly output "number of hospitalizations per year." It outputs a hazard ratio — a relative comparison. To convert that into the probability breakdown in the simulator, we applied a simple external calculation called a Poisson approximation. It's not in the notebook, but it's straightforward enough to explain here.

Step 1: What's the base rate?

In the NHANES sample, 15.7% of adults 50+ were hospitalized in the past year (238 out of 1,516). We treat this as the annual hospitalization rate for an average person in this group.

Step 2: Adjust for your activity profile

The Cox model gives you a relative risk — how much higher or lower your hazard is compared to a median person with median BMI, median steps, etc. If your relative risk is 1.5, you're 50% higher than average.

Your estimated annual rate = 0.157 × relative risk from Cox model Example: if your relative risk is 1.5 → rate = 0.157 × 1.5 = 0.236 expected hospitalizations/year

Step 3: Convert rate to probabilities (the Poisson part)

A rate of 0.236 doesn't directly tell you "what's the chance of 0, 1, or 2 visits?" The Poisson formula does that conversion. Think of it like this: if on average you'd expect 0.236 hospitalizations per year, what's the probability that you get exactly 0? Exactly 1? Exactly 2?

Let λ = your estimated annual rate (e.g. 0.236) P(0 hospital visits this year) = e^(−λ) → e^(−0.236) = 79% P(exactly 1 visit) = λ × e^(−λ) → 0.236 × 0.79 = 19% P(exactly 2 visits) = (λ²÷2) × e^(−λ) → (0.056÷2) × 0.79 = 2% P(3 or more) = 1 − all of the above = ~0.3%

The Poisson formula is just a well-known math pattern for counting rare events over time. It's the same math used for things like "how many calls does a call center get per hour?" or "how many cars pass a stoplight per minute?" We're applying it to hospitalization events.

Why this is approximate: The hospitalization data in NHANES is retrospective — it asks "were you hospitalized in the past year?" — not a forward prediction. The Poisson formula assumes events are independent and random, which hospitalizations are not. Treat these numbers as a rough scale for comparing high-activity vs. low-activity profiles, not as actual medical predictions.

6. How Is Blood Pressure Estimated? (And What is mmHg?)

The Cox model does not predict blood pressure. The BP number in the simulator is a separate rough estimate — not from the notebook's Cox model. Here's how it works and why we included it.

What is mmHg?

mmHg stands for "millimeters of mercury." It's the unit used to measure blood pressure, left over from when doctors used actual mercury in glass tubes to measure it. When a doctor says your BP is "130 over 80," the 130 is your systolic pressure in mmHg — the force of blood pushing against your artery walls when your heart beats. The 80 is diastolic — the pressure between beats. Higher numbers mean your heart is working harder to push blood through.

  • Under 120 mmHg — Normal
  • 120–129 — Elevated
  • 130–139 — Stage 1 Hypertension
  • 140+ — Stage 2 Hypertension (the threshold where doctors typically prescribe medication)

Where does the estimate come from?

In our NHANES dataset, the scatter plots in Part 1 of the notebook showed that people with more weekly steps tend to have lower systolic BP, and people with higher BMI tend to have higher BP. Those are real correlations in the data.

For the simulator, we used a simplified linear formula based on those observed relationships:

Estimated Systolic BP ≈ 130 + (BMI − 28.5) × 1.2 − (weekly steps − 38,000) × 0.00015 Where: 130 = approximate average BP for the median NHANES adult 50+ in this dataset 28.5 = median BMI in the dataset 38,000 = median weekly steps 1.2 = rough mmHg increase per BMI unit (from published literature) 0.00015 = rough mmHg decrease per additional step per week

This is not a medical formula — it's a simplified approximation to show the direction and rough scale of these associations. The real relationship is nonlinear, varies by age, sex, and medication use, and is confounded by many things not in this model. Do not use it to make any health decisions.

7. Why is the Model Inaccurate? (The Honest Version)

Problem 1: 7 days ≠ a lifetime

The assignment asked us to build "activity trajectories" — essentially, is someone's activity declining over months or years? A real trajectory would need measurements at age 55, 60, 65, 70. We have one week. We're calling it a trajectory, but it's really a cross-sectional snapshot.

Problem 2: The clock is backwards

We used age − 50 as the "time" axis in the Cox model. This treats age as if it were a follow-up period. But every person was measured at one point in time — we didn't actually follow anyone over years. It's a reasonable approximation, but it's not real longitudinal survival analysis.

Problem 3: Reverse causality

The hospitalization data is retrospective — it asks "were you hospitalized last year?" But the accelerometry was measured at roughly the same time. So a person who was hospitalized for a chronic illness might walk less because they're sick — not the other way around. Low activity could be a consequence of illness, not a predictor of it.

Problem 4: The proportional hazards assumption is violated

Cox regression assumes the hazard ratio between two people stays constant over time. Our model's assumption test flagged total_steps as a violator (p=0.012). That means the relationship between step count and hospitalization risk might change with age — maybe steps matter more at 60 than at 80. The model can't handle that.

Problem 5: BMI is doing most of the work

The strongest predictor in the model is BMI — a clinical measurement, not an accelerometry feature. The wearable data added modest value on top of BMI. If you removed all the accelerometry features and just kept BMI, the model wouldn't be that much worse.

Problem 6: Missing confounders

Lots of things predict hospitalization that we didn't include: chronic diseases (diabetes, heart disease), medications, smoking, prior hospitalization history. Not including them means our model is partly picking up on those hidden factors.

8. What Would Make It Better?

The right data: Multiple accelerometry waves, 2–5 years apart, with actual hospitalization dates. That would give real trajectories and real forward-looking predictions.

  • Longitudinal data: Measure the same people at age 55, 60, 65. Track whether activity declines, and whether that decline precedes hospitalization.
  • Time-varying covariates: Update BMI and activity at each measurement, not just once.
  • Clinical covariates: Add disease diagnoses, medications, smoking — things that confound the activity-hospitalization relationship.
  • Fix the PH violation: Bin total_steps into low/medium/high and stratify the model on it, rather than treating it as a continuous linear predictor.
  • Prospective design: Measure activity, then wait a year and record who was hospitalized. That eliminates the reverse causality problem.

What would a real clinical deployment look like?

Imagine a smartwatch that continuously tracks step count, sleep, and resting heart rate. Every week, it updates your risk score based on the past month of data. If your activity drops more than 30% below your personal baseline, it flags you for a check-in call. That requires: continuous data, personal baselines, and a model trained on actual follow-up outcomes — not a 7-day cross-sectional snapshot.

9. Glossary

  • Accelerometer — A sensor that measures movement and acceleration. Used to estimate steps, sedentary time, and sleep.
  • Cox regression — A statistical model for time-to-event data. Predicts hazard (instantaneous risk) as a function of covariates.
  • Hazard ratio (HR) — How much one group's risk differs from another's. HR = 2.0 means twice the risk.
  • C-index — How often the model ranks a higher-risk person above a lower-risk person. 0.5 = random, 1.0 = perfect.
  • Coefficient (β) — The weight the model assigns to each feature. Exponentiated to get the hazard ratio.
  • p-value — Probability that you'd see this effect by random chance if it weren't real. Below 0.05 = statistically significant.
  • Proportional hazards — The assumption that hazard ratios stay constant over time. Can be tested and sometimes violated.
  • Cross-sectional — Data collected at a single point in time. The opposite of longitudinal.
  • Longitudinal — Data collected from the same people over an extended period.
  • Confounding variable — A hidden variable that affects both your predictor and your outcome, making it look like there's a relationship when there may not be.
  • mmHg — Millimeters of mercury. The unit for blood pressure. 130 mmHg means the pressure is strong enough to push a column of mercury 130mm high. Leftover from old-style mercury manometers.
  • ROC curve — Receiver Operating Characteristic curve. A graph showing every possible threshold for a predictor and the tradeoff between catching real cases (sensitivity) and avoiding false alarms (specificity).
  • Youden's J — A formula (J = sensitivity + specificity − 1) for finding the single best threshold on a ROC curve. Used in Part 5 of the notebook to find the optimal step count cutoff.
  • Poisson distribution — A probability model for "how many times does a rare event happen per unit time?" Used in the simulator (not the notebook) to convert the Cox hazard rate into probabilities of 0, 1, 2+ events.
  • Sensitivity — Out of all the people who actually had the event (hospitalization), what fraction did the model correctly identify? High sensitivity = few missed cases.
  • Specificity — Out of all the people who did NOT have the event, what fraction did the model correctly leave unflgged? High specificity = few false alarms.
  • Reverse causality — When the outcome actually causes the predictor, not the other way around. Low activity after hospitalization looks like a risk factor, but may be a consequence.
