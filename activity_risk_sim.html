How This All Works — Plain English

This guide explains what we built, why we built it, and what's wrong with it. No prerequisites needed — if you can understand a recipe, you can understand this.

1. What Data Are We Using?

The data comes from NHANES — the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. Every few years, the CDC recruits thousands of Americans and does a full health checkup: blood tests, measurements, questionnaires. In 2005, they also handed out a device called an accelerometer.

What is an accelerometer?

An accelerometer is a tiny sensor that detects movement. You've got one in your phone — it's how your phone knows when you tilt it. The ones in this study were worn on the hip for 7 days straight, recording how much you moved every single minute.

From that minute-by-minute motion data, we can calculate things like: How many steps did you take? How long did you sit completely still? Did you sleep through the night?

The catch: This is only 7 days of data per person. A single week is not representative of someone's activity over months or years. That's one of the big reasons this model is limited.

Who is in the dataset?

We used 1,516 adults age 50–85. Of those, 238 (15.7%) reported being hospitalized in the past year. The accelerometry data was recorded at roughly the same time as — or after — the hospitalization, which matters a lot (more on this later).

2. What Features Did We Build?

The raw data is 15 million rows of minute-level motion readings. We can't feed that directly into a model — we need to summarize it into a few meaningful numbers per person. These summaries are called features.

Total Steps (weekly)

The total number of steps taken across all 7 wear days. Population median: about 46,500 steps/week (~6,600/day). Hospitalized patients in the data averaged 36,500/week vs. 51,800 for non-hospitalized — a 15,000-step gap that is statistically significant (t-test p<0.0001).

% Sedentary Time (pct_zero)

The fraction of all recorded minutes where the accelerometer detected zero movement, expressed as a percentage. Someone who sits at a desk all day might be 75% sedentary. The population median in NHANES adults 50+ is about 63%. Each 1% increase in sedentary time multiplies hospitalization hazard by 1.025 in the model.

Sleep Proxy ⭐ — the dominant predictor

The fraction of nighttime minutes (10pm–6am) where the device recorded zero movement. A value close to 1.0 means you were essentially still all night — usually meaning you were asleep. Population median is 0.960 — most people are nearly motionless at night.

This turned out to be the biggest single factor in the model — by a wide margin. The hazard ratio is 0.104, meaning a unit increase in sleep proxy drops hospitalization hazard to about 10% of baseline. The coefficient (−2.265) is roughly 40× larger in magnitude than the BMI coefficient. But it's tricky to interpret — a high sleep proxy could mean healthy deep sleep, OR it could mean someone who is too ill to move at night. See Section 7 for the full discussion.

BMI (Body Mass Index)

Your weight in kilograms divided by your height in meters squared. Categories: Under 18.5 = underweight, 18.5–25 = healthy, 25–30 = overweight, 30+ = obese. Population median: 28.05. BMI is the most statistically reliable predictor in our model (p<0.0001) — it shows up consistently regardless of what else is in the model.

Activity Slope

How your step count changed across the 7 days — were you more active on day 7 than day 1? This was designed to be the "trajectory" feature — capturing physical decline. It had no significant effect (p=0.504). With only 7 days, there's not enough data to detect meaningful trends.

3. What is a Cox Proportional Hazards Model?

A Cox model is a type of survival analysis. It was designed to answer questions like "how long until a patient dies?" — but it works for any time-to-event question. We're using it for: at what age does a person get hospitalized?

The basic idea

We treat each patient's age (minus 50) as the "time observed," and hospitalization as the event. The model asks: how does each person's risk of being hospitalized right now depend on their characteristics? That risk at any moment is called the hazard.

Hazard(person) = Baseline Hazard × e^(β₁×Steps + β₂×SedentaryTime + β₃×SleepProxy + β₄×BMI + ...) Where: β₁, β₂, β₃, β₄ = weights learned from data (the coefficients) e = Euler's number (~2.718) Baseline Hazard = average risk in the population (cancels out in comparisons)

What is exp(coef), the hazard ratio?

Each coefficient β gets exponentiated to produce a hazard ratio (HR):

  • HR = 1.0 → no effect on risk
  • HR = 1.5 → 50% higher risk per unit increase
  • HR = 0.5 → risk cut in half per unit increase

Why does total_steps have a positive coefficient? More steps should mean lower risk — but the coefficient is slightly positive. This is most likely due to multicollinearity: steps and sedentary time (pct_zero) are highly correlated. When the model already has pct_zero controlling for sedentary behavior, total_steps doesn't add much unique information and its coefficient becomes unreliable in direction. The step count threshold (35,000/week) from Part 5 of the notebook is still valid — it comes from using steps alone as a predictor, not from the full Cox model.

Reading the BMI row as an example

BMI coefficient = +0.05760. Hazard ratio = e^0.05760 = 1.059. That means for every 1 unit increase in BMI, your hospitalization hazard goes up by 5.9%. Going from BMI 25 to BMI 35 (10 units): 1.059^10 = 1.78× the hazard — nearly double the risk. And that's just from BMI alone, holding everything else constant.

What is the C-index?

The C-index measures how well the model ranks people by risk. Take any two people — one who got hospitalized, one who didn't. What fraction of the time did the model assign higher risk to the hospitalized person?

  • 0.50 = random guessing (coin flip)
  • 0.65+ = clinically useful
  • 1.00 = perfect

Our model got 0.639 — better than chance, but not reliable enough to act on clinically.

4. What Did the Cluster Analysis Tell Us?

We ran K-means clustering on patients' cardiovascular health markers (BP, BMI, diastolic BP, waist). We also clustered on activity patterns (peak hour, morning fraction, daily consistency). Both analyses pointed to the same conclusion.

There are two worlds, not a spectrum

The high-activity clusters (averaging 69,000–80,000 steps/week) had hospitalization rates of 8–8.5%. The low-activity clusters (30,000–38,000 steps/week) had rates of 19–22%. But here's what matters: the high-activity patients also had lower BMI, lower blood pressure, and smaller waist circumference. It wasn't just that they walked more.

The implication: You can't move someone from cluster 3 to cluster 2 just by telling them to take more steps. The healthiest patients aren't "people who walk more" — they're a fundamentally different metabolic profile. This raises the question of whether activity causes health, or whether health enables activity (see Section 7).

Does time of day matter?

We tested whether morning-dominant movers had better outcomes than night-dominant movers with the same total activity. Among patients within one standard deviation of average step count: night-active patients had 15.1% hospitalization rate, morning-active had 14.9%. The difference is essentially noise.

We also built an AR(1) model predicting tomorrow's steps by time block. Morning was the most predictable (R²=0.342), night the least (R²=0.292) — but all three blocks had similar predictability. Morning routines are habitual, night activity is noisy. Neither finding changes the health picture: volume matters, timing doesn't.

5. The Step Count Threshold — What is "Youden-Optimal"?

From Part 5 of the notebook: What weekly step count best separates people who were hospitalized from people who weren't?

ROC Curve — finding the best cutoff

If you draw a line and say "everyone below this step count is high risk," there's a tradeoff: set it too low and you miss real cases; set it too high and you flag everyone. The ROC curve plots every possible threshold, showing:

  • Sensitivity — of all hospitalized patients, what fraction did you correctly flag?
  • Specificity — of all non-hospitalized patients, what fraction did you leave alone?

Youden's J — picking the best point

J = Sensitivity + Specificity − 1 The threshold where J is highest balances catching real cases vs. avoiding false alarms as well as possible.

In our notebook, the Youden-optimal threshold came out to approximately 35,000 steps/week. Below that, hospitalization rates were noticeably higher. Above it, lower. The ROC AUC for steps alone was about 0.57 — barely better than random (0.50). The full Cox model reaches C-index 0.639 by combining all features.

6. How Does the Simulator Estimate Hospitalizations Per Year?

Honest disclaimer: The Cox model outputs a hazard ratio — a relative comparison. To get "number of hospitalizations per year," we apply a Poisson approximation. It's not in the notebook, but it's standard math for rare event counts.

Step 1: Base rate

15.7% of adults 50+ in the NHANES sample were hospitalized in the past year (238/1,516). This is our baseline annual rate for an average person.

Step 2: Adjust via Cox relative risk

The Cox model compares your activity profile to the median patient (median steps=46,455, median BMI=28.05, median pct_zero=62.9%, median sleep_proxy=0.960). Your relative risk scales the base rate up or down.

Your estimated rate λ = 0.157 × relative_risk Example: relative_risk = 1.5 → λ = 0.157 × 1.5 = 0.236 hospitalizations/year

Step 3: Poisson probabilities

P(0 visits this year) = e^(−λ) P(exactly 1 visit) = λ × e^(−λ) P(exactly 2 visits) = (λ²÷2) × e^(−λ) P(3 or more) = 1 − all of the above

The Poisson formula is the standard math for "how many times does a rare event happen per unit time?" Same math as: how many calls per hour to a call center, how many cars per minute at a stoplight.

Why this is approximate: Hospitalization data is retrospective — it asks "were you hospitalized last year?" The Poisson formula assumes events are independent and random, which hospitalizations are not. Treat these numbers as a rough scale for comparing profiles, not as actual medical predictions.

7. How Is Blood Pressure Estimated? (And What is mmHg?)

The Cox model does not predict blood pressure. The BP number is a separate rough estimate based on observed correlations in the NHANES data.

What is mmHg?

mmHg stands for "millimeters of mercury." Left over from when doctors used actual mercury in glass tubes. When your BP is "130 over 80," the 130 is your systolic pressure in mmHg — the force of blood against artery walls when your heart beats. Higher = heart working harder.

  • Under 120 mmHg — Normal
  • 120–129 — Elevated
  • 130–139 — Stage 1 Hypertension
  • 140+ — Stage 2 (where doctors typically prescribe medication)

Where does the estimate come from?

The scatter plots in Part 1 of the notebook show that people with more weekly steps tend to have lower systolic BP, and people with higher BMI tend to have higher BP — real correlations in the data. Average systolic BP in the dataset is 134.2 mmHg at median BMI (28.05) and median steps (46,455).

Estimated Systolic BP ≈ 134 + (BMI − 28.05) × 1.2 − (weekly steps − 46,455) × 0.00012 Where: 134 = observed mean BP in NHANES 50+ sample (from dataset) 28.05 = median BMI in dataset (from SIM PREP cell) 46,455 = median weekly steps in dataset (from SIM PREP cell) 1.2 = rough mmHg increase per BMI unit (published literature) 0.00012 = rough mmHg decrease per additional step/week

This is not a medical formula — it's a simplified approximation. The real relationship is nonlinear and confounded by many things not in this model.

8. Why Is the Model Inaccurate? (The Honest Version)

Problem 1: Reverse causality — the big one

The hospitalization data asks "were you hospitalized last year?" But the accelerometry was measured at roughly the same time. So a person hospitalized for a chronic illness might walk less because they're sick — not the other way around. Low activity could be a consequence of illness, not a predictor of it. The 15,000 step gap between groups is real, but we can't say which caused which.

Problem 2: Weak prediction overall

C-index = 0.639. The model correctly ranks a random hospitalized patient above a random non-hospitalized one only 63.9% of the time. For reference, a good clinical risk model is above 0.70.

Problem 3: Sleep proxy is confounded

Sleep proxy has by far the largest coefficient, but it's almost certainly confounded. Sick people sleep poorly and move around at night; they'd have a low sleep proxy because of their illness — not because poor sleep caused their hospitalization. We can't separate these effects.

Problem 4: 7 days ≠ a lifetime of behavior

One week of accelerometry doesn't capture long-term activity trends. Illness, travel, or bad weather during that specific week would distort the picture completely.

Problem 5: 50+ only

Ages 50–85 only. Cannot generalize to younger populations.

Problem 6: Activity slope is useless

The feature we designed to capture "is this person's activity declining?" — activity_slope — had essentially zero effect (coef = −8.75×10⁻⁵, p=0.504). Seven days is not enough data to detect a meaningful trend.

9. What Would Make It Better?

The right data: Multiple accelerometry waves, 2–5 years apart, with actual hospitalization dates recorded prospectively. That would give real trajectories and real forward-looking predictions.

  • Longitudinal design: Measure the same people at age 55, 60, 65. Track whether activity declines before hospitalization, not at the same time.
  • Prospective outcome: Measure activity first, then wait a year and record hospitalizations. Eliminates reverse causality.
  • Resting heart rate: NHANES 2005 doesn't include it. Heart rate would probably be the strongest predictor and is easy to measure with modern wearables.
  • Clinical confounders: Disease diagnoses, medications, smoking history — all predict hospitalization and all correlate with activity.
  • Personal baseline: Instead of comparing to the population median, compare each person to their own historical average. A 20% drop from your baseline is more informative than your absolute step count.

10. Glossary

  • Accelerometer — A sensor that measures movement. Used to estimate steps, sedentary time, and sleep.
  • Cox regression — A statistical model for time-to-event data. Predicts hazard as a function of covariates.
  • Hazard ratio (HR) — How much one group's risk differs from another's. HR = 2.0 means twice the risk.
  • C-index — How often the model ranks a higher-risk person above a lower-risk one. 0.5 = random, 1.0 = perfect.
  • Coefficient (β) — The weight the model assigns to each feature. Exponentiated to get the hazard ratio.
  • p-value — Probability you'd see this result by chance if there were no real effect. Below 0.05 = statistically significant.
  • Multicollinearity — When two predictors are highly correlated, making their individual coefficients unreliable. Steps and sedentary time are an example.
  • Cross-sectional — Data collected at a single point in time. The opposite of longitudinal.
  • Reverse causality — When the outcome actually causes the predictor. Low activity after hospitalization looks like a risk factor but may be a consequence.
  • mmHg — Millimeters of mercury. The unit for blood pressure. 130 mmHg = pressure strong enough to push a mercury column 130mm high.
  • ROC curve — A graph showing every possible decision threshold and the tradeoff between catching real cases (sensitivity) vs. avoiding false alarms (specificity).
  • Youden's J — J = sensitivity + specificity − 1. Used to find the single best threshold on a ROC curve. Used in Part 5 to find the optimal step cutoff (~35,000/week).
  • Poisson distribution — A model for "how many times does a rare event happen per unit time?" Used in the simulator to convert the Cox hazard rate into 0/1/2/3+ probabilities.
  • AR(1) model — Autoregressive model of order 1. Predicts today's value from yesterday's. Used to forecast next-day step counts by time block.
  • Sleep proxy — Fraction of nighttime minutes (10pm–6am) with zero accelerometer movement. High = still all night. The dominant predictor in our model, but confounded by illness.
  • K-means clustering — An algorithm that groups data points into K clusters by minimizing within-cluster distance. Used to find natural health archetypes in the patient data.
